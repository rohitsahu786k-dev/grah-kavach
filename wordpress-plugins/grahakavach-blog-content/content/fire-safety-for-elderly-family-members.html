Older family members are at higher risk in a fire. They may move more slowly, hear the alarm less well, take medicines that cause drowsiness, or find it hard to use a lock or a staircase. A good plan adjusts the home for them, so that getting out is as easy as possible.

Why older adults face higher risk

  • Slower reaction and walking speed, or the use of a stick, walker or wheelchair.
  • Reduced hearing, vision or sense of smell, so alarms and gas leaks are missed.
  • Medicines or sleep that make waking up in an emergency harder.
  • Loose clothing, and cooking or heating alone for long periods.

Make the escape route easy

  • Ground floor if possible. A bedroom near the exit means a shorter, safer way out.
  • Clear the path. Remove loose rugs, cables, stools and clutter between the bed, bathroom and door.
  • Easy locks. Use door and grille locks that can be opened without strength or a hidden key, and hang the keys in a fixed spot within reach.
  • Lighting. Add motion-sensor or night lights along the path.
  • Keep a phone and torch at the bedside, plus a list of emergency numbers in large print: 101 (fire), 112 (emergency), 102 or 108 (ambulance).

Detect danger earlier

  • Install a smoke alarm in the bedroom passage and test it monthly. Choose a loud one, and consider one with a flashing light if hearing is poor.
  • Make sure someone checks the gas regulator is off at night.
  • If an older person lives alone, arrange a daily call or a neighbour's check-in.

Cooking and heating safely

  • Encourage short sleeves and fitted clothing when cooking. Loose sarees, dupattas and shawls can catch flame.
  • Use a timer and never leave cooking unattended. Consider a fire blanket near the kitchen door. It is easy to use with two hands and needs no strength. See how to use it.
  • Keep room heaters at least a metre from beds, curtains and clothing, and switch them off before sleeping.
  • Keep pooja lamps, incense and candles on a stable metal tray, away from cloth and curtains. See common fire risk zones.

Medical oxygen and electrical equipment

If anyone uses medical oxygen, no flame, incense, matches or smoking should be anywhere near it, and heaters should be kept well away. Ask the supplier for guidance. Check the power cords of oxygen concentrators, nebulisers and heaters for damage, and do not overload sockets.

Practise the plan

  1. Agree who helps whom, and have a back-up person for each.
  2. Practise a night-time exit twice a year with the real lights off.
  3. Choose a meeting point outside the building where everyone gathers.
  4. Teach the older person one thing only: if there is fire or smoke, get out and stay out. Fighting a fire is for others to do.

Keep equipment where they can reach it

If an older person may need to use equipment, choose a lighter extinguisher and mount it at a low-to-mid height. An automatic fire ball gives protection even if nobody is able to act. See the complete home fire safety kit guide.
